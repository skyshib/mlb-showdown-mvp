How a card gets a number put on it

Five layers turn a printed card into a dollar bid. Each one reshapes the number the previous one produced, which is why three different quantities all end up being called “value”. Every constant and every figure below is read out of the engine at generation time.

1 · The card model — raw card to value points

src/rules/valuation.js

The only layer that looks at a card on its own, with no board and no roster around it. Hitters and pitchers do not share a formula and do not even read their charts the same way: a hitter's chart counts as a per-slot rate, a pitcher's as a total. So chart size matters for an arm and not for a bat.

Hitters

hitterValue = onBaseUsage(onBase) × lean × edge × 130 + glove(fielding, position) × 7 + legs(speed) × 1.5 onBaseUsage(ob) = mean over the reference staff [3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7] of clamp(ob − control, 0, 20) / 20 edge = chartRate × w_chart − (-1.35) lean = w_onBase / 20 (1 when persona-neutral)

On-base multiplies chart quality instead of adding to it, because on-base literally is the share of plate appearances settled on the batter's own card. And edge is measured against -1.35 — what a typical pitcher's chart is worth when his card is the one read — so a bat whose chart rates below that line gets negative benefit from reaching base more often. He would rather the pitcher's card were consulted.

The glove, by where he stands

unit(x) = rate × bend × (1 − e^(−x / bend)) unit'(x) = rate × e^(−x / bend) glove(r, pos) = unit(base[pos] + r) − unit(base[pos]) no bend → glove(r) = rate × r (the bend → ∞ case: a straight line)

A flat rate said a catcher's tenth point was worth his first, and that a point at short was worth a point in left. The WPA-per-162 sweep says otherwise, and says it the same way in all five clubs measured: the catcher's curve is steep below what clubs field and nearly flat above it, the infield bends the same way less sharply, and the outfield is the one unit that keeps paying all the way up.

Every next point is worth strictly less than the one before it, with no step and no corner: one smooth family covers all three, and the outfield is its bend → ∞ case rather than a separate rule. The catcher bends hardest because he IS his whole unit and so starts at the steep end of the curve, while an infielder's three team-mates have already carried the unit into the flat part before he arrives. The rates are the measured win curves rescaled to hold the deck's TOTAL glove value where the flat rate had it, so this redistributes fielding by position rather than re-weighting it against on-base and chart. Where the caller knows the seat a bat takes it passes it, so the glove is priced at the spot he stands in rather than the one printed on his card.

The legs, and the steal

legs(s) = max(0, s − 1) × 0.977 + max(0, s − 18)^2 × 0.195

Speed pays twice and the halves have different shapes. Taking the extra base on a hit is open to everyone and scales smoothly, so it stays linear. Stealing does not: the sim prices a steal at (speed − the catcher's fielding) / 20 and a runner only gets the green light once that clears break-even, so against an ordinary catcher nothing much below Speed A ever runs. That makes the top of the range worth far more than the middle. The knee sits at 18, the line the engine itself draws.

8 to 18 is worth 15; 18 to 28 is worth 44, 3.0× as much. A flat rate called them equal. These rates hold the deck's total speed value where the flat rate had it, so the elite runner's premium comes out of the middle of the range rather than being added on top.

Every weight is then multiplied by seeded noise of ±25%, keyed on seed:valuation:managerId:persona. This happens whatever the persona, so there is no such thing as “the” value of a card — only some manager's value of it. Two seats on the same board can be 1.67× apart on the same card before persona enters at all.

2 · The board re-deals every arm

pitcherRanking() · src/rules/draft.js

This is the layer that surprises people. It throws away layer 1's ordering of pitchers and keeps only its spacing. Bats pass through untouched.

batters = mean on-base and mean chart run value of the top 36 bats on the board advantage = share of d20 faces where d20 + control ≥ batters.onBase runsPerPa = advantage × chartRunValue(his chart) + (1 − advantage) × batters.runValue 1. sort the role's arms by runsPerPa, best first 2. sort their layer-1 values, highest first 3. hand the sorted values to the rate order

So the order comes from the engine's own hook rule — the real control × chart interaction the game plays — and the spacing comes from the card model. This is why an additive control-plus-chart valuation ranked arms worse than chart alone, and why re-pricing out types was a null: runsPerPa cannot tell a groundball from a flyball.

On this board: batters.onBase 10.89, batters.runValue 0.346.

Starter tilt

spread = sd of the board's starter runsPerPa tilt = 0.65 at spread ≤ 0.04, 1.00 at spread ≥ 0.05, linear between this board: spread 0.0347 → tilt 0.650

A board whose starters sit in a narrow band has no real ace, so money buys more on a bat or a reliever. A board with a genuine cliff pays full price. This board is on the narrow side, so every starter below is marked down 35%.

Reliever spacing

share = relievers who beat the fielded rotation's rate by > 0.02 runs/PA spacing = clamp(share / 0.3, 0, 1) then blend the rank values toward (runs saved × innings he would really throw) this board: 5 of 19 relievers (26.32%) → spacing 0.88

Printed points barely separate a 647-inning arm from a 29-inning one, which is how a computer once bid $119 on the best card in a room and $182 on a replacement-level one.

3 · Context — who is already standing there

Three readings sit on top of layer 2, all of them about the card's surroundings rather than the card.

The replacement floor

Per poolGroup — a bat's position, SP/RP for an arm. It is the lowest value at that group anywhere in the deck, then raised to the standing replacement's value where the room deals them. Pass on every catcher and you are handed that free card, not the worst catcher on the board, so he is the man a real catcher has to beat.

The glove he is actually credited with

asRostered(roster, bat).fielding = seatedGlove(roster + him) − seatedGlove(roster) DH seat = 0 glove · a non-first-baseman at first = −1

A bat is credited with the marginal glove he adds to the best nine, not his printed rating. Priced off the printed number instead, 29% of the bids on a bat paid for a glove the card never fields — a mean 2.1 fielding points too much, and as much as 11 on a surplus catcher.

seatedModelValue then totals the best nine with each bat valued at the glove of the slot he stands in, and pricingLineup pads any unfilled seat with the free man who would end up standing there before that total is taken.

The two branches are not interchangeable. The fills branch carries the urgency premium and prices through asRostered; the redundant branches price the marginal gain over the man who would otherwise play. Collapsing them into one — treating the nine as manned from the first lot, so no bat ever fills a hole — measured −0.30, −0.25, −1.31, −1.05 win points across four absolute cells against a clean null. The hole-filling premium is load-bearing.

Open questions

Known soft spots, with what is measured and what is not. This is the part to argue with.

The floor map keeps the wrong man

auctionWorth looks the floor up by poolGroup and the map keeps the highest standing replacement per key. Where two seats share a key — a DH replacement printed at a position — the floor is set by a card standing somewhere else, and every real card at that position is priced against him. Not measured.

The rotation is slotted by card points, not by value

lineupRankValue returns player.points, so a manager holding surplus starters can field a worse one. Re-slotting one live room by engine value read +1.0 / +1.5 / +1.1 / +1.0 across four seeds. One room only, and the equivalent change for the lineup previously lost to points, so it needs the harness before it means anything.

The gate and the price seat the roster differently

hitterTakesOpenSlot seats the roster in roster order; the price seats it point-maximally. The gate can therefore say a bat has no seat while the price seats him at DH anyway. Narrowed but not removed by pricingLineup. Not separately measured.

Nobody charges for the man being displaced

A redundant starter is priced as the margin over the manager's own weakest fielded arm, which is correct going forward and ignores the money already sunk in the arm he displaces. A computer can buy a rotation at need prices and then buy it again as upgrades. One live room put $305 of $1500 into three starters who never threw a pitch; trading them for two bats at the same board's rates was worth +8.3 win points.
